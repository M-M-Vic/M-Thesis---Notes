\section{Analysis of Model-\texorpdfstring{$B$}{B} (\texorpdfstring{$\gamma_1,\gamma_2>0$}{gamma1,gamma2>0}, \texorpdfstring{$\theta_1=\theta_2=0$}{theta1=theta2=0})}\label{sec:model_b}

In this section, we derive a general solution to the fundamental equation of Model-$B$. However, this is provided in terms of the unknown boundary function $P_y(y)$, which requires more advanced mathematical tools and is thus left open, but included for illustrative purposes to conclude that having derivative terms in $y$ in our fundamental equation is a non-trivial obstacle. So, this section not only shows the difficulty of Model-$B$ but also justifies not carrying out an analogous derivation for Model-$X$. Moreover, since jockeying breaks our Poisson arrivals and renewal structure, the probabilistic argument explained in \S~\ref{sssec:A:probabilistic} is no longer available.

Model-$B$ introduces bidirectional jockeying ($\gamma_1,\gamma_2>0$) and no abandonment ($\theta_1=\theta_2=0$). Figure~\ref{fig:model-b-queue} shows the queue layout.

\textbf{Stability.} $\rho = (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/\mu < 1$. Jockeying preserves the total customer count and does not affect this condition, as the arriving customers can also be re-allocated.

Setting $\theta_1=\theta_2=0$ in the general fundamental equation~\eqref{eq:gen:fundamental_equation} (Lemma~\ref{lem:gen:fundamental_equation}) removes both abandonment factors $(x-1)$ and $(y-1)$ and leaves the \emph{Model-$B$ fundamental equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    & \bigl[(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)xy - \mu y - \lambda_1 x^2 y - \lambda_2 x y^2\bigr]\,P(x,y) \\
    & \qquad + \gamma_1\,xy(x-y)\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}(x,y)
             + \gamma_2\,xy(y-x)\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}(x,y) \\
    & \qquad\qquad = \mu(x-y)\,P_y(y) - \mu\,x(1-y)\,\pi(0,0).
\end{split}
\label{eq:B:fundamental}
\end{equation}
This equation governs the entire analysis of the section, and we refer to it throughout in place of the general equation~\eqref{eq:gen:fundamental_equation}. Its only derivative terms are the two jockeying convection terms, both carrying the diagonal factor $(x-y)$ that makes the Method of Characteristics applicable.

The following result is stated as a corollary, so that it stays at the same hierarchy level as the equivalent result for other models. However, since this model is not solved, there is no theorem to it.
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:B:pi00}
    The empty-system probability $\pi_0$ and the empty-queues probability $\pi(0,0)$ of Model-$B$ coincide with those of the Model-$A$ baseline,
    \begin{equation}
        \pi_0 = 1-\rho, \qquad \pi(0,0) = \rho(1-\rho),
        \label{eq:B:pi00}
    \end{equation}
    and the diagonal of the PGF is $P(z,z)=\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)$.
\end{corollary}
The proof follows immediately by setting $x=y=z$ in the Model-$B$ fundamental equation~\eqref{eq:B:fundamental}: both jockeying terms, which carry the factor $(x-y)$, vanish, dragging the term $\mu(x-y)P_y(y)$ with them. The surviving diagonal equation is therefore \emph{identical} to that of Model-$A$ with $x=y=z$, so the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00} reduces to that of Corollary~\ref{cor:A:pi0}.


For Model-$B$ we do not provide a solution; however, working out the problem exposes the precise analytical obstacle, which we record as a claim. For the sake of readability, we will \emph{not} define here the auxiliary functions which will be defined throughout the proof. That being said, the following claim is made:
\begin{claim}\label{clm:B:volterra}
 Assume stability $\rho<1$. Along the characteristic lines $\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y=C_1$ of the Model-$B$ fundamental equation~\eqref{eq:B:fundamental}, parametrised by $y(x)=(C_1-\gamma_2 x)/\gamma_1$, the bivariate PGF of Model-$B$ admits the following integral representation:
    \begin{equation}
        P\bigl(x,y(x)\bigr) \;=\; P_h\bigl(x,y(x)\bigr)\int_{0}^{x}
        \frac{h\bigl(t;C_1\bigr)}{P_h\bigl(t,y(t)\bigr)}\,dt,
        \label{eq:B:integral_rep}
    \end{equation}
    where $P_h$ is the homogeneous solution and the forcing function $h$ depends linearly on the unknown boundary trace $P_y(y)=P(0,y)$. Evaluating \eqref{eq:B:integral_rep} along the diagonal $x=y=z$, where $P(z,z)=\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)$ is known from Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00}, reduces the determination of $P_y$ to a \emph{Volterra integral equation of the first kind}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F}(z) \;=\; \int_0^z \mathcal{K}(z,t)\,P_y\!\bigl(y(t;z)\bigr)\,dt.
        \label{eq:B:volterra}
    \end{equation}
\end{claim}
As said before, this model is therefore left open.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Claim~\ref{clm:B:volterra}]
    The proof consists of five steps: (i)~homogeneous solution via the Method of Characteristics, (ii)~ sign analysis of the exponent function $E$, (iii)~particular solution via the Method of Variation of Parameters, (iv)~determining the arbitrary function from the boundary condition, and (v)~ evaluation at $x=y$ yielding a Volterra-type equation.
    
    \medskip\noindent\textbf{Step~(i): Homogeneous solution via the Method of Characteristics.}
    
    The Model-$B$ fundamental equation~\eqref{eq:B:fundamental} is a first-order
    linear PDE in $P(x,y)$. Setting the
    right-hand side to zero and dividing by $y$ yields the homogeneous PDE
    \begin{equation*}
        \gamma_1 x(x-y)\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}
        - \gamma_2 x(x-y)\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}
        = -\bigl[(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)x - \mu - \lambda_1 x^2
                 - \lambda_2 xy\bigr]P.
    \end{equation*}
    Following the Method of Characteristics (\S\ref{sssec:moc}), we associate to this PDE the characteristic system
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{dx}{\gamma_1 x(x-y)}
        = \frac{dy}{-\gamma_2 x(x-y)}
        = \frac{dP}{-\bigl[(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)x - \mu - \lambda_1 x^2 - \lambda_2 xy\bigr]P}.
    \end{equation*}
    Taking the ratio of the first two differentials, the $x(x-y)$ factors cancel, giving $dx/\gamma_1 = dy/(-\gamma_2)$.  Integrating yields our characteristic constant:
    \begin{equation*}
        C_1 = \gamma_2 x + \gamma_1 y.
    \end{equation*}
    The characteristic curves are therefore the straight lines of slope $-\gamma_2/\gamma_1$ in the $(x,y)$-plane. We parametrise $y$ by setting $y(x) = (C_1-\gamma_2 x)/\gamma_1$. To extract the equation for $P$ along this curve, we need to define our coefficient $A(x,y)$:
    \begin{equation}
        A(x,y) := (\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)x - \mu - \lambda_1 x^2 - \lambda_2 xy.
        \label{eq:B:Adef}
    \end{equation}
    Substituting $y = (C_1-\gamma_2 x)/\gamma_1$ so that $\lambda_2 xy = (\lambda_2 C_1/\gamma_1)x - (\lambda_2\gamma_2/\gamma_1)x^2$, we obtain, collecting by powers of $x$,
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{split}
        A\bigl(x,y(x)\bigr)
        &= (\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)x - \mu - \lambda_1 x^2
           - \frac{\lambda_2 C_1}{\gamma_1}\,x + \frac{\lambda_2\gamma_2}{\gamma_1}\,x^2 \\
        &= -\frac{\gamma_1\lambda_1-\lambda_2\gamma_2}{\gamma_1}\,x^2
           + \frac{\gamma_1(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)-\lambda_2 C_1}{\gamma_1}\,x
           - \mu.
        \end{split}
    \end{align*}
    Similarly, $x - y(x) = [(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x-C_1]/\gamma_1$, so $\gamma_1 x(x-y(x)) = x[(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x-C_1]$.  The $dP/P$ equation from the characteristic system therefore now reads as follows:
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{dP}{P}
        &= \frac{-A(x,y(x))}{\gamma_1 x(x-y(x))}\,dx
         = \frac{(\gamma_1\lambda_1 - \lambda_2\gamma_2)x^2
                 - \bigl[\gamma_1(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu) - \lambda_2 C_1\bigr]x
                 + \gamma_1\mu}
                {\gamma_1 x\bigl[(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x - C_1\bigr]}\,dx.
    \end{align*}
    To integrate this, we decompose the integrand via partial fractions. Writing the integrand as $\tfrac{1}{\gamma_1}I(x)\,dx$,
    \begin{equation*}
             I(x) = K + \frac{M}{x} + \frac{N(C_1)}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x - C_1};
    \end{equation*}
    satisfying    
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{split}
             &K(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x^2
              + \bigl[M(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)+N-KC_1\bigr]x - MC_1 \\
             &\quad = \quad (\gamma_1\lambda_1-\lambda_2\gamma_2)x^2
             - \bigl[\gamma_1(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)-\lambda_2 C_1\bigr]x
              + \gamma_1\mu.
        \end{split}
    \end{align*}
    The $x^2$ and $x^0$ coefficient matches give $K$ and $M$ immediately; the $x^1$ match determines $N$ after simplification:
    \begin{equation}
        K = \frac{\gamma_1\lambda_1 - \lambda_2\gamma_2}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2},
        \qquad
        M = -\frac{\gamma_1\mu}{C_1},
        \qquad
        N(C_1) = \gamma_1\!\left[
            \frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2}C_1
            - (\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)
            + \frac{\mu(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}{C_1}
        \right].
        \label{eq:B:KMN}
    \end{equation}
    For $N$, the $x^1$ coefficient match gives
    \begin{align}
        \begin{split}
            N &= -\gamma_1(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu)+\lambda_2 C_1+KC_1-M(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) \\
              &= -\gamma_1(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu) + \lambda_2 C_1
                 + \frac{(\gamma_1\lambda_1-\lambda_2\gamma_2)\,C_1}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2}
                 + \frac{\gamma_1\mu(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}{C_1} \\
              &= -\gamma_1(\lambda+\mu)
                 + C_1\,\frac{\lambda_2(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)+\gamma_1\lambda_1-\lambda_2\gamma_2}
                             {\gamma_1+\gamma_2}
                 + \frac{\gamma_1\mu(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}{C_1} \\
              &= -\gamma_1(\lambda+\mu)
                 + \frac{\gamma_1\lambda}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2}\,C_1
                 + \frac{\gamma_1\mu(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}{C_1};
        \end{split}
        \nonumber
    \end{align}
    factoring $\gamma_1$ out recovers $N(C_1)$ from ~\eqref{eq:B:KMN}.
    
    Integrating $dP/P = (1/\gamma_1)\,I(x)\,dx$ term by term gives
    \begin{equation}
        \ln P = \frac{K}{\gamma_1}x - \frac{\mu}{C_1}\ln x
        + \frac{N(C_1)}{\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}\ln\bigl[(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x - C_1\bigr]
        + \ln f(C_1),
        \label{eq:B:lnP}
    \end{equation}
    where $f(C_1)$ is an arbitrary function of the characteristic constant.
    Now note that
    \begin{equation*}
        (\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x - C_1
        = (\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x - (\gamma_2 x + \gamma_1 y)
        = \gamma_1(x-y),
    \end{equation*}
    so $\ln[(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)x-C_1] = \ln\gamma_1 + \ln(x-y)$.
    
    Exponentiating \eqref{eq:B:lnP} term by term,
    \begin{equation*}
        P \;=\; f(C_1)\,
        \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{K}{\gamma_1}x\Bigr)\cdot
        x^{-\mu/C_1}\cdot
        \gamma_1^{\,N(C_1)/(\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2))}\,
        (x-y)^{\,N(C_1)/(\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2))};
    \end{equation*}
    the factor $\gamma_1^{\,N(C_1)/(\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2))}$ depends on $x$ and $y$ only through $C_1$ and can be absorbed, together with $f$, into a redefined arbitrary function $F(C_1)$:
    \begin{equation*}
        P \;=\; F(C_1) \cdot
        \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{K}{\gamma_1}x\Bigr)\cdot
        x^{-\mu/C_1}\cdot
        (x-y)^{\,N(C_1)/(\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2))}.
    \end{equation*}
    
    Reading off the exponent of $(x-y)$, with $C_1=\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y$, defines
    \begin{equation}
        E(x,y) := \frac{N(C_1)}{\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}
        = \frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)^2}(\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y)
        - \frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\mu}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2}
        + \frac{\mu}{\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y}.
        \label{eq:B:E}
    \end{equation}
    So our general homogeneous solution reads as follows:
    \begin{equation}
        P_h(x,y) = F(\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y)
        \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{\gamma_1\lambda_1-\lambda_2\gamma_2}{\gamma_1(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}x\right)
        \cdot x^{-\mu/(\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y)}
        \cdot (x-y)^{E(x,y)},
        \label{eq:B:Ph}
    \end{equation}
    where $F$ is an arbitrary and differentiable function of the characteristic constant $C_1$; we will see that it must be zero in Equation~\eqref{eq:B:integral_rep_body}.
    
    
    \medskip\noindent\textbf{Step~(ii): The exponent $E(x,y)$ is strictly positive in $(0,1)^2$.}
    
    As one of the building blocks for this proof, we need to see that $E(x,y)$ is strictly positive in $(0,1)^2$
    
    Recall $\lambda = \lambda_1+\lambda_2$. Along any fixed characteristic, $C_1 = \gamma_2 x + \gamma_1 y$ is constant, so the exponent function~\eqref{eq:B:E} depends only on $C_1$, which ranges over $(0,\gamma_1+\gamma_2)$ as $(x,y)\in(0,1)^2$:
    \begin{equation*}
        E(C_1) = 
        \frac{\lambda}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)^2}\,C_1
        - \frac{\lambda+\mu}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2}
        + \frac{\mu}{C_1}.
    \end{equation*}
    
    Showing that $E(C_1)>0$ on $C_1\in(0,\gamma_1+\gamma_2)$, follows from two facts. First, $E$ vanishes at the right endpoint,
    \begin{equation*}
        E(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)
        = \frac{\lambda-(\lambda+\mu)+\mu}{\gamma_1+\gamma_2} = 0.
    \end{equation*}
    Second, $E$ is strictly decreasing in $(0,\gamma_1+\gamma_2]$, using $u\le\gamma_1+\gamma_2$ and $\mu>\lambda$ (i.e.\ $\rho<1$):
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{split}
            E'(u) &= \frac{\lambda}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)^2} - \frac{\mu}{u^2}
        \end{split}\\
        \begin{split}
            &\le \frac{\lambda}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)^2} - \frac{\mu}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)^2}
            = \frac{\lambda-\mu}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)^2} < 0,
        \end{split}
    \end{align*}
    Since $E$ decreases strictly to $0$ at its right endpoint, it is strictly positive too for every point in the left, so:
    \begin{equation*}
        E(C_1) > E(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) = 0 \qquad\text{for all } C_1\in(0,\gamma_1+\gamma_2),
    \end{equation*}
    that is, $E(x,y) > 0$ for all $(x,y)\in(0,1)^2$. For this proof, the crucial boundary behaviour is that $E \to 0$ as $(x,y) \to (1,1)$; the blow-up caused by $\mu/C_1$ as $C_1 \to 0^+$ is irrelevant.
    
    \medskip\noindent\textbf{Step~(iii): Particular solution via variation of parameters.}
    
    This step consists in upgrading the homogeneous solution $P_h$ (~\eqref{eq:B:Ph}) with its inhomogeneous part, along the characteristic.
    We start by rewriting our fundamental equation for Model-$B$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:B:fundamental}). The coefficient of $P$ on the LHS can be written as $yA(x,y)$ —where $A(x,y)$ is defined as in  Step~(1) (Equation~\eqref{eq:B:Adef})—; and we define the RHS of the fundamental equation as follows:
    \begin{equation}
        G(x,y) := \mu(x-y)\,P_y(y) - \mu\,x(1-y)\,\pi(0,0);
        \label{eq:B:Gdef}
    \end{equation}
    this forcing involves the unknown boundary trace $P_y(y)=P(0,y)$ together with the known constant $\pi(0,0)=\rho(1-\rho)$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00}. Separating the terms that involve derivatives and the ones that do not, w have
    \begin{equation*}
        \gamma_1 xy(x-y)\,\partial_x P + \gamma_2 xy(y-x)\,\partial_y P = -\,y\,A(x,y)\,P + G(x,y),
    \end{equation*}
    which is the convection form~\eqref{eq:ode:pde} of the Method of Characteristics.
    So, along the characteristic $C_1=\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y$ found in Step~(i)
    (parametrised by $y(x)=(C_1-\gamma_2 x)/\gamma_1$), \S\ref{sssec:moc} reduces it to a
    first-order linear ODE of the form~\eqref{eq:ode:vop_standard},
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{dP}{dx} = f(x;C_1)\,P + h(x;C_1).
        \label{eq:B:ode_char}
    \end{equation}
    The homogeneous part $\frac{dP}{dx}=f(x;C_1)\,P$ is $P_h$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:B:Ph}), so there is no need to write $f$ out; and its forcing function is the right-hand side divided by the coefficient of
    $\partial_x P$,
    \begin{equation}
        h(x;C_1)=\frac{G\bigl(x,y(x)\bigr)}{\gamma_1\,x\,y(x)\,\bigl(x-y(x)\bigr)}.
        \label{eq:B:hdef}
    \end{equation}
    Equation~\eqref{eq:B:ode_char} is an instance of~\eqref{eq:ode:vop_standard} with the known homogeneous solution $P_h$. So the variation-of-parameters formula~\eqref{eq:ode:vop_general} of \S\ref{sssec:vop} delivers its general solution, with the arbitrary constant becoming a function $F(C_1)$ of the characteristic
    label:
    \begin{equation}
        P(x,y(x)) = P_h(x,y(x))\!\left[F(C_1) + \int_{x_0}^{x} \frac{h(t;C_1)}{P_h(t,y(t))}\,dt\right].
        \label{eq:B:variation}
    \end{equation}
    
    % ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    \medskip\noindent\textbf{Step~4: Pinning the free function from the boundary condition at $x=0$.}
    
    Along any characteristic with constant $C_1>0$, the endpoint at $x=0$ is $y(0)=C_1/\gamma_1$, giving
    \begin{equation*}
        P\!\left(0,\,\frac{C_1}{\gamma_1}\right) = P_y\!\left(\frac{C_1}{\gamma_1}\right),
    \end{equation*}
    which is finite since it is a (marginal) PGF.  However, $P_h$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:B:Ph}) contains the factor $x^{-\mu/C_1}\to+\infty$ as $x\to0^+$ (since $\mu/C_1>0$ for $C_1>0$), while the integral in~\eqref{eq:B:variation} vanishes as $x_0\to0$, since the integration interval shrinks. Therefore
    \begin{equation*}
        \lim_{x\to0^+}P(x,y(x))
        = \lim_{x\to0^+}P_h(x,y(x))\cdot F(C_1)
        = +\infty\cdot F(C_1),
    \end{equation*}
    which can only be finite if $F(C_1)=0$.  Setting $x_0=0$ and $F(C_1)=0$ in~\eqref{eq:B:variation} yields the integral representation of Claim~\ref{clm:B:volterra}:
    \begin{equation}
        P(x,y(x)) = P_h(x,y(x))\int_{0}^{x} \frac{h(t;C_1)}{P_h(t,y(t))}\,dt.
        \label{eq:B:integral_rep_body}
    \end{equation}
    
    % ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    \medskip\noindent\textbf{Step~5: Reduction to the Volterra equation.}
    
    By Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00} the empty-state probabilities are $\pi_0=1-\rho$ and
    $\pi(0,0)=\rho(1-\rho)$, with diagonal value $P(z,z)=\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)$; these
    are the inputs to the diagonal evaluation below.
    
    We simplify the forcing function $h$ on the characteristic.  Substituting the
    inhomogeneity $G$ of~\eqref{eq:B:Gdef} into the forcing $h$ of~\eqref{eq:B:hdef}:
    \begin{align}
        h(t;C_1)
        &= \frac{\mu(t-y(t))\,P_y(y(t)) - \mu t(1-y(t))\,\pi(0,0)}
                 {\gamma_1\,t\,y(t)\,(t-y(t))} \nonumber\\
        &= \frac{\mu\,P_y(y(t))}{\gamma_1\,t\,y(t)}
           - \frac{\mu(1-y(t))\,\pi(0,0)}{\gamma_1\,y(t)\,(t-y(t))}.
        \label{eq:B:h_intermediate}
    \end{align}
    We will evaluate~\eqref{eq:B:integral_rep_body} along the diagonal characteristic,
    which passes through $(z,z)$ and has $C_1=(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)z$. The parametrisation
    along that curve is
    \begin{equation}
        y(t;z)=\frac{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)z-\gamma_2 t}{\gamma_1},
        \label{eq:B:ydiag}
    \end{equation}
    so $y(0;z)=(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)z/\gamma_1>z$ and $y(z;z)=z$. Therefore $y(t;z)>t$
    for all $t\in[0,z)$, which means $t-y(t;z)<0$ throughout the integration interval.
    Writing $t-y(t;z)=-(y(t;z)-t)$ in the denominator of~\eqref{eq:B:h_intermediate}:
    \begin{equation}
        h\bigl(t;\,(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)z\bigr)
        = \frac{\mu}{\gamma_1\,t\,y(t;z)}\,P_y\!\bigl(y(t;z)\bigr)
        + \frac{\mu\,(1-y(t;z))}{\gamma_1\,y(t;z)\,(y(t;z)-t)}\,\pi(0,0).
        \label{eq:B:h_simplified}
    \end{equation}
    
    As established in Step~2, $E>0$, so the homogeneous factor $(x-y)^E$ vanishes on the
    diagonal and $P_h\bigl(x,y(x;z)\bigr)\to0$ as $x\to z^-$; simultaneously the integrand
    in~\eqref{eq:B:integral_rep_body} develops a non-integrable singularity
    $\sim(y(t;z)-t)^{-1-E}$ as $t\to z^-$, so the integral diverges. Their product is the
    finite $0\cdot\infty$ limit
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{\pi(0,0)}{1-\rho z}
        = \lim_{x\to z^-} P_h\bigl(x,y(x;z)\bigr)
          \int_0^x \frac{h\bigl(t;(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)z\bigr)}{P_h\bigl(t,y(t;z)\bigr)}\,dt,
    \end{equation*}
    whose value is $P(z,z)=\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00}.
    Substituting~\eqref{eq:B:h_simplified} and separating the term containing the unknown
    $P_y$ from the known remainder, this limit splits as
    \begin{align}
        \frac{\pi(0,0)}{1-\rho z}
        &= \lim_{x\to z^-}\frac{\mu\,P_h\bigl(x,y(x;z)\bigr)}{\gamma_1}
           \int_0^x \frac{P_y\bigl(y(t;z)\bigr)}{t\cdot y(t;z)\cdot P_h\bigl(t,y(t;z)\bigr)}\,dt
        \nonumber\\
        &\quad
        + \lim_{x\to z^-}\frac{\mu\,P_h\bigl(x,y(x;z)\bigr)\,\pi(0,0)}{\gamma_1}
           \int_0^x \frac{1-y(t;z)}{y(t;z)\cdot(y(t;z)-t)\cdot P_h\bigl(t,y(t;z)\bigr)}\,dt.
        \label{eq:B:split}
    \end{align}
    We now evaluate the two limits in~\eqref{eq:B:split} one by one; this both
    clarifies in what sense the kernel pairing below is to be read and provides an
    independent consistency check of the representation. The geometry on the diagonal
    characteristic is exactly linear,
    \begin{equation}
        y(t;z)-t \;=\; \frac{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)z-\gamma_2 t}{\gamma_1}\;-\;t
        \;=\; \frac{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)}{\gamma_1}\,(z-t),
        \label{eq:B:exact_linear}
    \end{equation}
    and along this characteristic ($C_1=(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z$) the exponent of Step~2 takes the
    closed form
    \begin{equation*}
        E_z \;:=\; E((\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z)
        \;=\; \frac{\lambda z^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)z+\mu}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z}
        \;=\; \frac{(\mu-\lambda z)(1-z)}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z}\;>\;0,
        \qquad z\in(0,1).
    \end{equation*}
    Every factor of $P_h$ in~\eqref{eq:B:Ph} other than the exponential, the power of
    $x$, and $(x-y)^{E}$ depends on $(x,y)$ only through $C_1$, so along the
    characteristic the homogeneous ratio is \emph{exact}:
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{P_h\bigl(x,y(x;z)\bigr)}{P_h\bigl(t,y(t;z)\bigr)}
        \;=\; R(x,t)\,\biggl(\frac{z-x}{z-t}\biggr)^{\!E_z},
        \qquad
        R(x,t):=e^{\frac{K}{\gamma_1}(x-t)}\,\Bigl(\frac{t}{x}\Bigr)^{\!\mu/((\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z)},
        \label{eq:B:ratio_exact}
    \end{equation}
    with $R$ smooth and $R(z,z)=1$. Substituting~\eqref{eq:B:ratio_exact} into the
    \emph{second} limit of~\eqref{eq:B:split} and using~\eqref{eq:B:exact_linear}, its
    integrand behaves as $w(z)\,(z-t)^{-1-E_z}$ near $t=z$, with
    $w(z)=\mu\,\pi(0,0)\,(1-z)/((\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z)$; the truncated integral diverges like
    $(z-x)^{-E_z}/E_z$, the prefactor vanishes like $(z-x)^{E_z}$, and the product
    converges:
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{split}
            \lim_{x\to z^-}\;(z-x)^{E_z}\!\int^{x}\! w(z)\,(z-t)^{-1-E_z}\,dt
            \;&=\; \frac{w(z)}{E_z}
            \;=\; \frac{\mu\,\pi(0,0)\,(1-z)}{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z}\cdot
                  \frac{(\gamma_1+\gamma_2) z}{(\mu-\lambda z)(1-z)} \\
            &=\; \frac{\mu\,\pi(0,0)}{\mu-\lambda z}
            \;=\; \frac{\pi(0,0)}{1-\rho z}\,.
        \end{split}
    \end{align*}
    The second limit alone thus reproduces the full diagonal value --- an independent
    confirmation that the representation~\eqref{eq:B:integral_rep_body} is consistent
    with Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00}. The \emph{first} limit, by contrast, vanishes: its
    integrand behaves as $(z-t)^{-E_z}$ near $t=z$, so for $E_z<1$ the integral
    converges and is annihilated by the vanishing prefactor $(z-x)^{E_z}$, while for
    $E_z\ge1$ the truncated integral grows only like $(z-x)^{1-E_z}$ (logarithmically
    at $E_z=1$) and the product is still $O\bigl((z-x)^{\min(E_z,1)}\ln(z-x)\bigr)\to0$.
    Hence \emph{at leading order the limit~\eqref{eq:B:split} reduces to the identity
    $\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)=\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)$ and carries no information about $P_y$}:
    the dependence on the boundary function enters only at the next order in $(z-x)$.
    Each limit is accordingly a \emph{Hadamard finite part}: writing
    $P_h^{\,\mathrm{diag}}(z):=\lim_{x\to z^-}P_h\bigl(x,y(x;z)\bigr)$ (which vanishes
    pointwise), the kernel pairing below is the extraction of the
    truncation-independent, subleading part of the divergent integrals, and neither
    factor is meaningful in isolation.
    % TODO(citation): add a standard reference for Hadamard finite-part integrals
    % (e.g. Hadamard, Lectures on Cauchy's Problem, 1923) -- the bibliography currently
    % lacks a suitable entry; comment only, no PDF output.
    The kernel and data so extracted are
    \begin{align}
        \mathcal{K}(z,t)&:=\frac{\mu\,P_h^{\,\mathrm{diag}}(z)}
            {\gamma_1\,t\,y(t;z)\,P_h\bigl(t,y(t;z)\bigr)},
            \label{eq:B:kernel}\\[4pt]
        \mathcal{F}(z)&:=\frac{\pi(0,0)}{1-\rho z}-\mathcal{B}(z),
            \qquad
            \mathcal{B}(z):=\frac{\mu\,P_h^{\,\mathrm{diag}}(z)\,\pi(0,0)}{\gamma_1}
            \int_0^z\frac{1-y(t;z)}
            {y(t;z)\,\bigl(y(t;z)-t\bigr)\,P_h\bigl(t,y(t;z)\bigr)}\,dt,
            \label{eq:B:Fdef}
    \end{align}
    the kernel $\mathcal{K}$ and the known term $\mathcal{B}$ \emph{to be read through
    the regularised limit~\eqref{eq:B:split} rather than pointwise}. With these,
    equation~\eqref{eq:B:split} is exactly the Volterra integral
    equation~\eqref{eq:B:volterra} of the first kind, proving the claim. The finite-part reading is not a decoration.
    As the limit-by-limit analysis shows, the displayed $\mathcal{K}$ and $\mathcal{B}$
    are formal --- the prefactor $P_h^{\,\mathrm{diag}}(z)$ vanishes pointwise --- and
    the equation for $P_y$ lives entirely at subleading order in $(z-x)$. Giving that
    extraction a rigorous meaning, whether by matched asymptotics near $t=z$ or by a
    change of variable removing the $(z-t)^{-1-E_z}$ singularity before passing to the
    limit, is itself the analytical obstruction: it is this step, together with the
    fact that the kernel $\mathcal{K}$ depends on $P_h$ through both arguments and does
    not simplify for general $(\gamma_1,\gamma_2)$, that places Model-$B$ beyond the
    scope of this thesis. Equation~\eqref{eq:B:volterra} is recorded as the precise form of
    the open problem, not solved here; resolving it would require Volterra inversion
    methods together with the regularisation just described.
\end{proof}

Model-$B$ is therefore left open.  Its role in the hierarchy is
structural: it identifies the precise obstruction~---~the Volterra equation
for $P_y$~---~that the tractable special cases $B_2$ and $C_2$ circumvent, each
by a different mechanism.  Setting $\gamma_2=0$ collapses the characteristic
family from a two-parameter set of oblique lines to a one-parameter set of
vertical lines, degenerating the PDE to an ODE in $x$ alone; the boundary
function $P_y(y)$ is then pinned by an analyticity argument at the interior
singular point $x=y$.  Model-$C_2$ removes jockeying entirely and pins $P_y$ by
a boundedness argument at the boundary of the domain.  Both specialisations are
analysed in the following sections.

\begin{remark}[The full model $X$ is \emph{a fortiori} intractable]\label{rem:B:modelX}
Model-$B$ already exhibits the central obstruction of this work: even with
abandonment switched off, pinning the boundary function $P_y(y)$~---~and hence
the joint PGF $P(x,y)$~---~still reduces to the first-kind Volterra
equation~\eqref{eq:B:volterra}, which has no closed form. Reinstating
abandonment ($\theta_1,\theta_2>0$) to recover the full parent model,
Model-$X$, can only deepen the difficulty, and it does so in two ways.

First, the characteristics cease to be straight lines. For
$\theta_1=\theta_2=0$ the characteristic slope is the constant
$-\gamma_2/\gamma_1$, giving the lines $\gamma_2 x+\gamma_1 y=C_1$ of
Claim~\ref{clm:B:volterra}. Once $\theta_i>0$ it becomes a genuine ratio of
affine forms,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{-\gamma_2 x+(\gamma_2+\theta_2)\,y-\theta_2}
                       {(\gamma_1+\theta_1)\,x-\gamma_1 y-\theta_1},
\end{equation*}
whose integral curves are level sets of a product of two \emph{non-integer}
powers of linear forms in $(x,y)$~---~transcendental curves, not straight lines.
Along them $y(x)$ is only implicitly defined, so the homogeneous solution
$P_h$ becomes a non-elementary quadrature and the Volterra kernel loses the
explicit form it keeps for Model-$B$.

Second, the diagonal anchor is lost. For Model-$B$ the diagonal value
$P(z,z)=\pi(0,0)/(1-\rho z)$ is known outright (Corollary~\ref{cor:B:pi00}) and
supplies the forcing of~\eqref{eq:B:volterra}; under abandonment, setting
$x=y=z$ in the fundamental equation no longer isolates $P(z,z)$, leaving a
surviving diagonal-gradient term
$[\theta_1\partial_x P+\theta_2\partial_y P]_{(z,z)}$, so the forcing is itself
coupled to the unknown solution. Model-$X$ is therefore left open. Model-$B$ is
the simplest model in which the obstruction already appears in full, and the
sections that follow retreat to specialisations that remove it.
\end{remark}
